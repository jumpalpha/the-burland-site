## About this episode

Episode 4 is about lava country -- the volcanic landscape that stretches across Southern Idaho and shapes everything from the terrain to the water supply. Most visitors come to Eastern Idaho for Yellowstone and the Tetons. They drive right past ancient lava fields, cinder cones, and atomic-age history without realizing what they are. This episode tells them what they are missing.

## About The Burland (brief reminder)

The Burland is a restored 1912 building in St. Anthony, Idaho, used for family reunions, weddings, and group stays. It sits at the edge of the Snake River Plain, where the valley floor gives way to some of the most dramatic volcanic geology in North America. This episode is about the landscape right outside the door.

## Why Eastern Idaho looks like this

The Snake River Plain is a broad, flat arc across southern Idaho, cutting northeast to southwest. It is the scar left by the North American plate moving over the Yellowstone hotspot -- the same volcanic engine that powers Yellowstone's geysers. As the plate moved, the hotspot punched through it in sequence, leaving a trail of ancient calderas and lava flows. The lava fields visitors see today are some of the youngest on the continent: some erupted as recently as two thousand years ago.

The lava plain is not a dead landscape. It is a giant sponge. Precipitation soaks through the porous basalt and feeds one of the largest aquifers in North America, the Eastern Snake Plain Aquifer. That aquifer is the reason the Henry's Fork runs cold and clear year-round. It is the reason the farms of Eastern Idaho grow what they grow. The water that pours out of Big Springs, north of Island Park, fell as snow on the mountains and percolated through the lava for decades before emerging as the Henry's Fork.

## Menan Buttes

The Menan Buttes are two volcanic cones rising out of the farmland north of Rexburg. They are rootless cones -- formed when a lava flow hit the river and flashed the water into steam, sending the eruption straight up. The North Butte, the larger one, has a trail to the crater rim. The hike is short and steep. At the top, the view takes in the Snake River Plain, the Tetons to the east, and the river below. The inside of the crater is a bowl, completely hidden from the outside. The lava is red and black and chunky -- different in texture and color from the flat fields around it.

The Menan Buttes are a designated Natural Landmark. They are geologically unusual even by Idaho standards: very few rootless cones of this size exist anywhere.

Practical note: the trail to the North Butte rim is a short hike. The footing is loose in places. It is not stroller-accessible but is manageable for active older adults.

## St. Anthony Sand Dunes

The St. Anthony Sand Dunes, eight miles west of The Burland, seem like they should not exist: a large field of white sand dunes in the middle of lava and farmland. They do exist because of the same geological history. Wind has deposited and shifted sand eroded from the Snake River Plain over thousands of years. The result is a dune system large enough for ATVs, horses, sandboarding, and kids who want to run.

Egin Lakes, beside the dunes, is a series of ponds fed by the aquifer. They are cold and clear and good for a cool-off after the sand.

The dunes are a Bureau of Land Management recreation area. Motorized use (ATVs, dirt bikes) and non-motorized use (walking, horses, sandboards) share the space.

## Mesa Falls and the Henry's Fork Canyon

Mesa Falls sits on the Mesa Falls Scenic Byway between Ashton and Macks Inn. The Henry's Fork drops off a basalt shelf -- volcanic rock from the same lava country -- in two falls: Upper Mesa Falls (114 feet) and Lower Mesa Falls. The falls exist because the river has cut down through the lava plain. What looks like a simple waterfall is actually the river carving its way through hundreds of feet of layered basalt.

Upper Mesa Falls has an accessible boardwalk with close views of the falls. The historic ranger station at the top has been restored as a visitor facility. Lower Mesa Falls requires a steep trail and is visited less. Both are on the same byway, a few miles apart.

The drive between Ashton and Macks Inn on the byway is scenic in its own right -- volcanic rim country, old-growth forest, and the canyon below.

## Hell's Half Acre Lava Field

Hell's Half Acre is a lava flow right off Interstate 15, about an hour southwest of St. Anthony. It is a convenient stop on the way to or from the airport or Craters of the Moon. A short paved trail leads into the flow from the roadside. The lava here is pahoehoe and aa -- two textures that form from the same eruption at different temperatures and flow speeds. The pahoehoe is ropy and smooth-looking, the aa is sharp and broken. Both are black and new-looking. This eruption happened roughly 6,100 years ago.

Hell's Half Acre is not a destination on its own for most families, but as a 20-minute stop on a drive, it is a quick and genuine introduction to lava country.

## EBR-I Atomic Museum

EBR-I -- Experimental Breeder Reactor 1 -- sits on the flat lava plain southwest of Idaho Falls, surrounded by nothing. It was the first place in the world where electricity was generated from nuclear energy, on December 20, 1951. The original reactor building still stands. Visitors can walk through it, see the original reactor core, and read the story of the team that did it.

The museum is free and operated by the Department of Energy. It is open seasonally -- generally Memorial Day through Labor Day. The exhibits are more interesting than the name suggests. The story is partly scientific and partly human: a small team, a remote desert location, and a moment that changed what was possible.

For a family with kids who are curious about science, EBR-I is the right amount of strange and historical. For a family doing a Craters of the Moon day, EBR-I is on the route and worth the stop.

## Craters of the Moon National Monument

Craters of the Moon is an hour and a half southwest of St. Anthony, in the central Snake River Plain. It is the most visited lava destination in the region and the one that justifies a full-day trip.

What it is: a lava field unlike anything most visitors have seen. Cinder cones, lava tubes, spatter cones, and tree mold caves cover an area the size of a small state. The park road loops through the monument and stops at multiple features.

### What to see

**Inferno Cone:** A cinder cone with a trail to the top. The view from the top takes in the entire monument, the surrounding plain, and the mountains on the horizon. It is a short, steep climb on loose cinders. Worth it.

**Lava tube caves:** Several lava tubes are open to visitors. Indian Tunnel is large enough to walk through without a flashlight. The other caves -- Dewdrop, Boy Scout, Beauty -- are darker and require a flashlight or headlamp. Closed-toe shoes with good grip are essential inside. The caves are cool year-round -- bring a layer.

**Spatter cones:** A short walk from the road to a cluster of small cones built by molten lava spattering out of a vent. They look like a row of broken chimneys.

**Tree molds:** Where a lava flow surrounded standing trees, the trees burned away and left their shapes in the solidified rock. The molds are walkable on a short loop.

### Practical notes

- Bring water. The monument is open and exposed. Heat in summer is real.
- Closed-toe shoes with grip are required for the caves and strongly recommended everywhere else. The lava is sharp.
- The visitor center at the entrance has exhibits, a bookstore, and restrooms.
- The park charges an entry fee. An America the Beautiful pass covers it.
- The road through the monument is paved and accessible by any vehicle.

### For families

The caves are the highlight for kids. Indian Tunnel is accessible to most ages with minimal gear. The smaller caves add adventure for older kids and teens. Inferno Cone is worth the climb if the group has the energy. A full Craters day -- including EBR-I on the way -- is a long day from St. Anthony, but a memorable one.

## Building a lava-country day

**Option A: Close to home, half day.** Morning at the Sand Dunes, then Menan Buttes in the afternoon. Both are within 30 minutes of The Burland. Pair with a swim at Egin Lakes. Easy for all ages.

**Option B: Mesa Falls and the byway.** Drive the Mesa Falls Scenic Byway: Warm River for a picnic, then Upper and Lower Mesa Falls, then back through Ashton. This is a half-day trip with good scenery and a dramatic waterfall. Add Box Canyon or the Henry's Fork for the anglers in the group.

**Option C: Craters full day.** Leave early. Stop at Hell's Half Acre on the way. Continue to EBR-I for 45 minutes. Continue to Craters of the Moon for the afternoon: Inferno Cone, the caves, and the spatter cones. This is a long day but a full one. Bring lunch, water, and closed-toe shoes for everyone.

## A note on what guests expect

Most guests coming to The Burland are thinking about Yellowstone and the Tetons. The lava country is a surprise. It is older, stranger, and closer than they expect. The sand dunes are eight miles away. The lava fields at Menan Buttes are visible from the valley. Craters of the Moon is a full day but a different kind of full day -- not the crowds of Yellowstone, not the dramatic peaks of the Tetons, but something genuinely other. For reunion groups that have done Yellowstone before, lava country is often the discovery of the trip.
